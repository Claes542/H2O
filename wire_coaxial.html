The wire: a kernel chain inside a cylinder of electrons
A central chain of kernels, the electrons standing off it at radius dr, and optionally an insulating wall outside. The sea is slid along the wire and the energy recorded. E(δ) is the pinning; its amplitude is the resistance.
m = 1 is a plain H-chain — one proton, one electron per site, the system used throughout this study, with the electron displaced radially instead of sitting on its proton. That is the case to run first: it is chemically real, it is the cheapest, and at dr in the plane it reproduces ring_slide.html exactly, whose periodicity check has already passed. m > 1 builds a genuine cylindrical shell, at the cost of a bare +m kernel with no core — an elaboration, not the test.
Why it is bent into a loop. A straight wire has ends, and sliding the sea off the end is an edge artefact rather than transport. Closed into a ring, sliding by one full site spacing returns an identical configuration, so E(δ=1) must equal E(δ=0) exactly — a check the calculation cannot fake. A wire in a circuit is a loop in any case.
The prediction being tested. A periodic chain of spacing a has its lowest Fourier component at G = 2π/a, and that component decays as e−G·dr away from the chain. So the pinning should fall exponentially with the standoff, by a factor e2π/a ≈ 17 per a0 at a = 2.2. An electron cylinder standing off the chain sees a smooth tube even though the chain is a lattice — which is why a nanotube conducts ballistically. The page prints measured against predicted.
The insulating wall is the box face, and costs nothing to add. The outermost domains run to the edge of the simulation box with free boundaries — which is exactly a zero-flux Neumann wall, the framework's own convention. So confinement is one parameter, box, not a new construction. It has been at half-width 15 a0 throughout this study, which is why the sea could spread: the corona had a wall, just a very distant one.
This is better than a sheath of atoms, which was the previous attempt: real atoms bring their own electrons, which hybridise with the sea and confound the measurement. A Neumann wall confines without adding chemistry. &nw=N still builds the atom sheath if wanted.
And it is the work function, in its cheapest form. A Neumann wall reflects perfectly at no cost; a Dirichlet wall (ψ=0) would cost energy and push density inward; Robin between them is a finite work function. The same β dial as the interface — so a Neumann box is the zero-work-function wire.
What decides where the cylinder sits. The chain pulls it in, the wall pushes it in, and what holds it out is degeneracy pressure — which at C=0 this framework does not have. So the cylinder may collapse onto the chain, which is the maximum-pinning configuration. If it does, the pinning is a consequence of the missing Fermi pressure rather than a separate defect.
Bare kernels are the worst case, and that matters for the 323 meV. A bare +1 is the most strongly corrugating potential a lattice can have. Real ion cores are screened by their core electrons — the Ashcroft empty-core radius is 1.67 a0 for sodium — so the sea in a real wire never sees a point charge. Note too that the corona run, which came out flat with no resistance, used neutral H, while the slide run that gave 323 meV used bare protons: the same screening confound flagged earlier, arriving from a new direction.
&rc=0.7 supplies a core. The kernel carries +m, one electron is bound close in at radius rc, and the remaining m−1 go to the sea — so the sea sees an effective +(m−1) softened by the core rather than a bare charge. With m=2&rc=0.7 that is an alkali wire: screened core, one valence electron, neutral. Hydrogen has no core, so m=1&rc=0 remains the honest metallic-hydrogen case.
