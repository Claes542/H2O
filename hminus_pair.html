H− + H : the smallest transfer there is
Two protons, three electrons. The left kernel carries two domains in a 1+1 shell arrangement — a tight inner electron and a diffuse outer one, which is what H− actually is (affinity 0.75 eV, the outer electron barely held). The right kernel carries one. Net charge −1.
How the shell split is made. Two domains on the same kernel with different initZeff: the tight trial density wins the inner cells, the diffuse one wins the outer shell, so the radial split emerges from the initialisation rather than being imposed. No new machinery, and every domain still carries exactly one unit of charge.
What is actually being measured. The symmetric state is not a rival ground state — it is the transition state between "electron on the left atom" and "electron on the right", which are degenerate by symmetry. So E(symmetric) − E(localised) is the hopping barrier. Small means facile transfer; large means a trapped carrier. For hopping at room temperature the barrier is of order kBT = 0.001 Ha, so the tolerance is set a hundred times tighter (2×10−5) and both cases are run at identical settings so systematics cancel in the difference. If the two agree within tolerance that is the answer, not a failure: no resolvable barrier.
The two runs. Run both and compare the converged energies: &mode=left seeds the outer electron on one atom — H− bound to H, the extra charge localised. &mode=both seeds it centred and diffuse — H2 surrounded by an electron, the extra charge wrapping the molecule. Both are legitimate three-domain states; the free boundary relaxes each to its own minimum. Whichever is lower is what the framework says an extra electron does in a molecule — sit on one atom, or spread over both. That is localisation against delocalisation in the smallest system that can show it, and it is the conduction question in miniature.
What is being watched. Free boundaries are on, so cells change hands wherever a neighbouring domain has the higher density — the solver's own mechanism, which does rearrange partitions. With bias = 0 the two kernels are identical and the ground state should share the extra electron. With bias > 0 the right kernel is made slightly more attractive and the outer electron should shift toward it. The question is how little bias it takes.
Prior question: does the carrier exist? The outer electron confines the inner one, since domains cannot overlap — which is why the left atom looks compressed. That costs energy, and real H− is bound by only 0.75 eV. If the confinement costs more than the outer electron gains, H− does not bind and there is nothing to transport. Run &noextra for the H + H reference at the same separation and compare: binding requires E(H−+H) < E(H+H).
Floor check. For two protons and three electrons at this separation the lowest available total energy is about −1.21 Ha (H2 at −1.17 plus at most ~1 eV of electron affinity). Anything appreciably below that is a spurious state, not a deep one — typically the carrier having claimed the cells at a nucleus and sitting in the unscreened well.
